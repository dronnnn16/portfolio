Chapter 8

Introduction to Trigonometry

Every right triangle has three sides. Divide one side by another and you get a ratio. Those ratios depend only on the angle, not on how big the triangle is.

8.1Name the three sides

The names change depending on which angle you are standing at. Tap an angle and watch.

Sides here are 3, 4 and 5. Notice how sin and cos swap when you switch angles.

  • Hypotenuse: the longest side, opposite the 90° angle. It never changes.
  • Perpendicular: the side facing your angle.
  • Base: the side touching your angle that isn't the hypotenuse.

Pandit Badri Prasad / Har Har Bole Read the first letters downwards in pairs: P/H, B/H, P/B. That gives sin, cos, tan in order. Flip them for cosec, sec, cot.

8.3Flips and the tan shortcut

\(\operatorname{cosec} A = \dfrac{1}{\sin A}\)\(\sec A = \dfrac{1}{\cos A}\)\(\cot A = \dfrac{1}{\tan A}\)

Divide sin by cos and the hypotenuse cancels out:

\[\tan A = \frac{\sin A}{\cos A} \qquad \cot A = \frac{\cos A}{\sin A}\]

For an acute angle, \(\sin A\) and \(\cos A\) are always between 0 and 1, because the hypotenuse is the longest side.

8.5Three identities

Pythagoras says \(\cP^2 + \cB^2 = \cH^2\). Divide that by different sides and you get all three:

IdentityHow you get it
\(\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1\)divide by \(\cH^2\)
\(1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A\)divide by \(\cB^2\)
\(1 + \cot^2 A = \operatorname{cosec}^2 A\)divide by \(\cP^2\)

You can move terms across too: \(\sin^2 A = 1 - \cos^2 A\) and \(\sec^2 A - \tan^2 A = 1\).

\(\sin^2 A\) means \((\sin A)^2\). Square the answer, not the angle.

8.6Two worked examples

If \(\tan A = \frac43\), find all the other ratios.

  1. \(\tan A = \dfrac{\cP}{\cB} = \dfrac43\), so take \(\cP = 4k\), \(\cB = 3k\).
  2. Pythagoras: \(\cH^2 = (4k)^2 + (3k)^2 = 25k^2\), so \(\cH = 5k\).
  3. \(\sin A = \frac{4}{5},\; \cos A = \frac{3}{5},\; \operatorname{cosec} A = \frac{5}{4},\; \sec A = \frac{5}{3},\; \cot A = \frac{3}{4}\).

\(\sin A = \frac45\), \(\cos A = \frac35\), the rest are flips

Evaluate \(\dfrac{5\cos^2 60^\circ + 4\sec^2 30^\circ - \tan^2 45^\circ}{\sin^2 30^\circ + \cos^2 30^\circ}\)

  1. The bottom is \(\sin^2 30^\circ + \cos^2 30^\circ = 1\) (identity!).
  2. Top: \(5\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 + 4\left(\tfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\right)^2 - 1^2 = \tfrac54 + \tfrac{16}{3} - 1\).
  3. Common denominator 12: \(\tfrac{15}{12} + \tfrac{64}{12} - \tfrac{12}{12} = \tfrac{67}{12}\).

\(\dfrac{67}{12}\)

Chapter 9

Some Applications of Trigonometry

Heights and distances. Draw a right triangle from the real picture, mark what you know, then use one ratio to find what you don't.

9.1Looking up, looking down

  • Line of sight: the straight line from your eye to the object.
  • Angle of elevation: you look up. The angle between the horizontal and the line of sight.
  • Angle of depression: you look down. Still measured from the horizontal, never from the wall.

The angle of depression from the top equals the angle of elevation from the bottom. They're alternate angles between two parallel horizontal lines.

9.2Which ratio do I use?

Look at the two things in the question: the one you know and the one you want. Pick the ratio that holds both.

Know and wantUse
height and distance on the ground\(\tan\theta = \dfrac{\cP}{\cB}\)
height and slant length (string, ladder, rope)\(\sin\theta = \dfrac{\cP}{\cH}\)
distance on the ground and slant length\(\cos\theta = \dfrac{\cB}{\cH}\)

Most tower questions are tan questions. Height and ground distance show up far more often than slant lengths.

9.3Two worked examples

From a point 15 m away from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of its top is 60°. Find the height of the tower.

  1. Know: ground distance = 15 m. Want: height \(h\). That's tan.
  2. \(\tan 60^\circ = \dfrac{h}{15}\)
  3. \(\sqrt3 = \dfrac{h}{15} \Rightarrow h = 15\sqrt3\)

\(h = 15\sqrt3\) m \(\approx 25.98\) m

A 10 m ladder leans against a wall and makes 60° with the ground. How high up the wall does it reach?

  1. Know: ladder (slant) = 10 m. Want: height \(h\). That's sin.
  2. \(\sin 60^\circ = \dfrac{h}{10}\)
  3. \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \dfrac{h}{10} \Rightarrow h = 5\sqrt3\)

\(h = 5\sqrt3\) m \(\approx 8.66\) m

Common mistakes

  • Swapping perpendicular and base. They depend on which angle you're at; check again for every new angle.
  • Reading \(\sin A\) as "sin × A". It's one value, the ratio for angle A.
  • Writing \(\sin^2 A\) as \(\sin (A^2)\). It means \((\sin A)^2\).
  • Saying \(\tan 90^\circ = 0\). It is not defined.
  • Getting \(\sin A\) or \(\cos A\) bigger than 1. Impossible, so recheck your sides.
  • Measuring the angle of depression from the vertical wall instead of the horizontal.
  • Forgetting units (m) in the final answer.

✍Questions with hints

Solve in your notebook first. Open the hint only if you're stuck, and the solution only to check.

Q1If \(\sin A = \frac34\), find \(\cos A\) and \(\tan A\).Ch 8

Take \(\cP = 3k\) and \(\cH = 4k\). Find the base with Pythagoras.
  1. \(\sin A = \frac{\cP}{\cH} = \frac34\), so \(\cP = 3k,\ \cH = 4k\).
  2. \(\cB^2 = (4k)^2 - (3k)^2 = 16k^2 - 9k^2 = 7k^2\), so \(\cB = \sqrt7\,k\).
  3. \(\cos A = \frac{\cB}{\cH} = \frac{\sqrt7}{4}\), \(\tan A = \frac{\cP}{\cB} = \frac{3}{\sqrt7} = \frac{3\sqrt7}{7}\).

\(\cos A = \frac{\sqrt7}{4}\), \(\tan A = \frac{3}{\sqrt7}\)

Q2Evaluate \(2\tan^2 45^\circ + \cos^2 30^\circ - \sin^2 60^\circ\).Ch 8

\(\cos 30^\circ\) and \(\sin 60^\circ\) are the same number. What happens to the last two terms?
  1. \(\tan 45^\circ = 1\), \(\cos 30^\circ = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\), \(\sin 60^\circ = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\).
  2. \(2(1)^2 + \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)^2 = 2 + \frac34 - \frac34\).

\(= 2\)

Q3Evaluate \(\sin 60^\circ \cos 30^\circ + \sin 30^\circ \cos 60^\circ\).Ch 8

Put in the values from the table and multiply each pair first.
  1. \(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}{2} + \frac12\cdot\frac12\)
  2. \(= \frac34 + \frac14\)

\(= 1\)

Q4Using \(\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1\), prove that \(1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A\).Ch 8

Divide every term by \(\cos^2 A\).
  1. Start with \(\sin^2 A + \cos^2 A = 1\).
  2. Divide each term by \(\cos^2 A\) (allowed when \(\cos A \ne 0\), i.e. \(A \ne 90^\circ\)): \(\dfrac{\sin^2 A}{\cos^2 A} + \dfrac{\cos^2 A}{\cos^2 A} = \dfrac{1}{\cos^2 A}\).
  3. \(\dfrac{\sin A}{\cos A} = \tan A\) and \(\dfrac{1}{\cos A} = \sec A\), so \(\tan^2 A + 1 = \sec^2 A\).

\(1 + \tan^2 A = \sec^2 A\). Hence proved.

Q5If \(15\cot A = 8\), find \(\sin A\) and \(\sec A\).Ch 8

\(\cot A = \frac{8}{15} = \frac{\cB}{\cP}\). Find the hypotenuse; 8, 15, 17 is a Pythagorean triple.
  1. \(\cot A = \frac{8}{15}\), so \(\cB = 8k,\ \cP = 15k\).
  2. \(\cH^2 = 64k^2 + 225k^2 = 289k^2\), so \(\cH = 17k\).
  3. \(\sin A = \frac{\cP}{\cH} = \frac{15}{17}\), \(\sec A = \frac{\cH}{\cB} = \frac{17}{8}\).

\(\sin A = \frac{15}{17}\), \(\sec A = \frac{17}{8}\)

Q6A kite is flying at a height of 60 m. Its string makes 60° with the ground. Find the length of the string (assume it is straight).Ch 9

You know the height and want the string (slant). Which ratio has P and H?
  1. Let the string be \(L\). \(\sin 60^\circ = \dfrac{60}{L}\).
  2. \(\dfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \dfrac{60}{L} \Rightarrow L = \dfrac{120}{\sqrt3}\).
  3. Rationalise: \(\dfrac{120}{\sqrt3}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt3}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{120\sqrt3}{3} = 40\sqrt3\).

\(L = 40\sqrt3\) m \(\approx 69.28\) m

Q7From a point 30 m from the foot of a tower, the angle of elevation of its top is 30°. Find the height of the tower.Ch 9

Height and ground distance: that's tan. \(\tan 30^\circ = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\).
  1. \(\tan 30^\circ = \dfrac{h}{30}\)
  2. \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{h}{30} \Rightarrow h = \dfrac{30}{\sqrt3}\).
  3. Rationalise: \(\dfrac{30\sqrt3}{3} = 10\sqrt3\).

\(h = 10\sqrt3\) m \(\approx 17.32\) m

Q8From the top of a 75 m high lighthouse, the angle of depression of a ship is 30°. How far is the ship from the foot of the lighthouse?Ch 9

The angle of elevation of the lighthouse top from the ship is also 30°. Now it's a normal tan question.
  1. Angle of depression = angle of elevation from the ship = 30° (alternate angles).
  2. Let the distance be \(d\). \(\tan 30^\circ = \dfrac{75}{d}\).
  3. \(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt3} = \dfrac{75}{d} \Rightarrow d = 75\sqrt3\).

\(d = 75\sqrt3\) m \(\approx 129.9\) m
